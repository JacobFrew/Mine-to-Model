What quantum computing really is

A normal computer stores everything as bits, each either 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses qubits, which follow the rules of physics at the scale of single atoms. With the right program, a quantum computer makes the wrong answers cancel each other out and the right answer grow stronger. That makes it extremely fast at a few specific kinds of problems, not a faster laptop.

1

Superposition

A qubit can be in a blend of 0 and 1 until you measure it. Think of a coin spinning in the air instead of lying heads or tails.

2

Entanglement

Qubits can be linked so that measuring one tells you about the others. Adding one qubit doubles the space of possibilities: 300 linked qubits describe more combinations than there are atoms in the universe.

3

Interference

Like noise-cancelling headphones, a quantum program arranges for wrong answers to cancel out and right ones to add up. Only a few known algorithms do this well, which is why quantum helps with some problems and not others.

4

Why it's so hard

Qubits are fragile. Heat, vibration or stray light knocks them out of their state ("noise"). That's why many must sit near absolute zero, and why today's best machines still make an error about once every 1,000 operations.

5

Physical vs. logical qubits

The fix is error correction: group many physical qubits into one reliable "logical" qubit. It may take roughly 100 to 1,000 physical qubits per logical one. Useful machines need hundreds to thousands of logical qubits, so headline qubit counts can mislead.

6

Where we are in 2026

Google showed in 2024 that errors can shrink as machines grow, and in 2025 the first checkable "quantum advantage" results appeared. No one has yet solved a valuable business problem faster than a supercomputer. Most leaders target 2029 for large error-corrected machines.

What it should be good for

  • Chemistry and materials: simulating molecules for drugs, batteries, fertilizer and catalysts. Nature is quantum, so this is the most natural fit.
  • Breaking today's encryption: a large enough machine could crack the RSA and elliptic-curve codes that protect the internet (see Q-Day below).
  • Some optimization and finance: routing, scheduling and risk models. Gains here are less proven.
  • Better sensors and clocks: the same physics makes ultra-precise navigation and timing, which some companies already sell.

What it won't do

  • Replace your laptop, phone or Nvidia GPUs. Quantum computers will work alongside supercomputers, like a specialized co-processor.
  • Speed up everyday software, video, web browsing or training AI models in general.
  • Make money for most of these companies soon. Revenue today comes mainly from governments and research labs buying machines and cloud time.

          Six ways to play it

          The same industry pays different companies at different times. Toolmakers and big tech get paid now from other businesses; the pure plays only pay off if useful machines arrive on schedule. Matching the bet to how long you can wait matters as much as picking the company.

          Q-Day: the encryption problem

          "Q-Day" is the day a quantum computer can break the encryption that protects bank logins, Bitcoin wallets and government secrets. It's the clearest real-world reason governments are spending on quantum, and on defending against it.

          <1M

          noisy qubits could break RSA-2048 in under a week

          Google's Craig Gidney (May 2025), down from 20 million in 2019. 2026 papers claim far fewer, though none has been built.

          28–49%

          chance of a code-breaking machine within 10 years

          Global Risk Institute's 2026 expert survey, the highest it has ever recorded; 69% of experts put it at 50% or more within 15 years.

          Now

          "Harvest now, decrypt later"

          Spies can copy encrypted data today and unlock it once a big enough machine exists, which is why the switch has to start years early.

          2024

          new encryption standards are ready

          NIST finished its first post-quantum standards (ML-KEM and two signature schemes) in August 2024 and picked HQC as a backup in 2025.

          2027–35

          deadlines to switch

          NSA requires post-quantum support in new systems from 2027 and everywhere by 2035; NIST deprecates RSA and ECC in 2030; the EU targets 2030 for high-risk systems.

          Hype check: the risks

          Things to keep in mind before buying any quantum stock.

          ~$1.4B

          The whole industry's revenue is small

          QED-C estimates about $1.4B of quantum computing revenue in 2025, rising to about $3B by 2028. Listed quantum companies are worth many times that.

          ~$895M

          Insiders have been selling

          Insiders at IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave and Quantum Computing Inc. sold roughly $840–930M of stock over several years through mid-2026. Some of it is routine tax selling on stock pay.

          Dilution

          New shares keep coming

          Pure plays fund their losses by selling stock; IonQ and QUBT built multi-billion-dollar cash piles that way. The government stakes are also new shares, sold at a discount.

          2025

          Even experts flip-flop on timing

          Nvidia's Jensen Huang said in January 2025 that useful quantum was 15–20 years away (the stocks crashed), then said in March that he'd been wrong.

          Disputed

          Claims get challenged

          D-Wave's 2025 "supremacy" result and Microsoft's Majorana qubits were both questioned by outside researchers. Wait for independent confirmation.

          Targets

          Analyst targets lag

          Many targets were set near the highs, before the July selloff, and some stocks have only one to four analysts. A +100% "upside" here says more about the stale target than the future.
